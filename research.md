# Portfolio Research: How Senior Product Designers at Big Tech Present Their Work

Goal: design a professional, text-and-design-heavy portfolio for a **Senior Mobile Developer who researches and validates** (audience: founders & product leads), consistent with the "Ink & Lime" brand.

## 2. Patterns: what they all have in common

### Home page
1. **One sentence about who you are, in the first person.** No slogans. E.g. *"I'm a software designer living in San Francisco, currently making AI products at Notion."*
2. **Work as a list, not a showcase grid.** Title + one-line description (often a question or an outcome). Few or no big images.
3. **Writing next to the work.** Almost every senior/design-engineer site lists essays or notes. Writing signals thinking.
4. **Quiet, document-like design.** Lots of white space, one typeface family, restrained colour, no animations or hero illustrations. The work and the words carry it.
5. **Small number of projects.** 3–5 deep case studies; everything else goes into a short "other work" or "projects" list.
6. **Contact is simple:** an email with copy, LinkedIn, X/GitHub.

### Case study
1. **Context first:** what the product is, why the project existed, your role, team, timeline, all in plain words.
2. **A clear, plain-language problem** (what was broken, for whom).
3. **The decision trail:** what you tried, what you rejected and why, the trade-offs. NN/g's survey of 204 hiring managers: show the messy process, constraints and candidate solutions that weren't chosen.
4. **Real artifacts as evidence:** screens, flows, research notes, prototypes, each with a **numbered caption** that explains it (Bethany Heck uses fig 1–35).
5. **Outcome:** a measured result if you have one; otherwise what shipped, what's being tracked, and what you learned. "A redesign that shipped without measurable impact reads like a concept project."
6. **Credit the team.** Senior people name collaborators; it signals honesty and leadership.
7. **Length:** about 800–1,500 words plus visuals. One focused decision told well beats a five-slide process deck. Some add a "jump to solution" link for skimmers.

### What senior-level reviewers reward (2025–26)
- **Judgment over process:** trade-offs (scope vs time, user need vs business goal), not double-diamond diagrams.
- **Verifiable, shipped work:** live links, real users, real constraints.
- **Depth in one problem space** over broad range.
- **Thoughtful AI use**, not AI-generated sameness.
- **Fewer, deeper projects:** "Three strong case studies beat seven mediocre ones."

## 3. Why my first case-study page didn't work

| What I did | What top portfolios do instead |
|---|---|
| A dark marketing-style hero, like a SaaS landing page | A calm document: title, one-paragraph summary, role and timeline as plain text |
| Big "stat cards" up front | Numbers appear **inside the story**, where they're earned, plus one outcome line |
| **No visuals of the actual app** | Screens, flows and research artifacts are the main evidence, with captions |
| Generic section labels ("01 · The problem") | Plain-language headings that say what happened ("New users were asked to commit before they saw any value") |
| Placeholder name and invented content | First-person voice, real project, real collaborators |

## 4. What this means for your portfolio

**Positioning line (home page):**
> I'm a senior mobile developer who researches and validates before building, so the apps I ship are the ones people keep.

**Site structure:**
1. **Home**: intro sentence + 3 featured case studies (title, one-line question/outcome, platform, year) + writing + contact
2. **Case studies** (3–5): each built on the template below
3. **Writing**: LinkedIn posts expanded into essays (teardowns, validation stories)
4. **About**: work history, how I work, tools (incl. AI), photo
5. **Now building**: your own app, built in public (waitlist link)

**Case-study template:**
1. Title (outcome-led) + one-paragraph summary
2. Facts row in plain text: Role · Team · Timeline · Platform · Status (shipped / tracking)
3. The problem: who, what was broken, how we knew (research)
4. What we learned: 1–2 insights, a user quote, the evidence
5. Options we considered: 2–3 directions, why one won (trade-offs)
6. Validation: prototype test, what changed
7. What shipped: annotated screens (fig. 1, 2, 3…)
8. Outcome & next: numbers if available; otherwise what's tracked and the decision it will drive
9. Credits & reflection: team names, what I'd do differently

**Visual direction (keep the brand, change the feel):**
- Mostly **Paper** (light) surfaces; **Ink** only for the site header/footer or a single cover image, not a full-width marketing hero.
- One reading column (~680px) with wide margins; figures can break out wider.
- Lime only for links, highlights and status; never decoration.
- Real app screens inside neutral device frames with numbered captions.
