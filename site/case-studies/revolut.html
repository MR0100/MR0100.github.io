Revolut makes everyday money effortless, then goes quiet on your worst day

Independent study. Not affiliated with or endorsed by Revolut. Public sources only, every number linked. The proposal screens are my own concept, built as an unbranded prototype. Anything I couldn't verify is labelled Hypothesis.

I expected to find that Revolut had added so many products that the everyday jobs were getting buried. The evidence said I was wrong. In 280 recent "most helpful" App Store reviews, only 1 complained about finding things, and none of the 65 negative ones did. The anger sits in a rarer moment: when an account or payment is restricted. 24 of the 65 negative reviews describe money that was held, blocked or closed (18 clear cases, plus 6 borderline ones such as declined payments or funds held pending an address check), and the most common complaint is not "why" but "when" and "what next". The law can stop a bank saying why. It doesn't stop it saying what happens next. So I designed a Case Tracker for restricted accounts, built it as a clickable prototype, and wrote the test that would prove or kill it.

The question I started with was the wrong one

Revolut ended 2025 with 68.3 million retail customers and £4.5bn of revenue (Annual Report 2025, pp. 4 and 12). Most of that revenue (card payments, interest, subscriptions, wealth) depends on one behaviour: people keeping their salary and savings in the app. Revolut says it is chasing the "primary account" role, and it launched its biggest redesign to win it (Revolut, Oct 2023). For a primary account, the metric that matters is trust.

My starting angle was super-app overload: stocks, crypto, travel and eSIMs crowding out the basics. Before designing anything, I pulled Apple's public "most helpful" review feeds for the UK, Ireland and the US (700 unique reviews), kept the 280 dated 1 Jan 2025 to 8 Oct 2026, and read all 65 one- and two-star reviews by hand.

Clutter barely exists in that data. One review in 280 (4★, Ireland, Feb 2026) said things like a simple transfer "are not where I would expect to find". None of the 65 negative reviews was about clutter or too many features. Many five-star reviews praise the opposite. I dropped the angle.

A bias warning: "most helpful" favours long, emotional reviews, so these counts show which themes exist, not how common they are across 68 million customers. A pull of the most recent UK reviews was 95% five-star (report §3). Most people are happy day to day.

The restricted-account reviews read alike:

"no clear explanation of what is happening, no indication of how or when the issue will be resolved, and no proper point of contact" (1★, Ireland, 2 Oct 2026)

"No customer support rep can give me any information about when this process will be done." (1★, US, 31 Dec 2025)

So the question changed from "is the app too busy?" to "what does the app tell you when your money is held?"

Fair to Revolut first

Restrictions are often a legal duty, not a choice. Revolut calls them "a last resort" and says it can't always share the reason (Revolut blog, via search snippet). UK "tipping-off" rules can stop any bank explaining why an account was frozen (Monzo explainer). Revolut was fined €3.5m in Lithuania in 2025 for weak anti-money-laundering monitoring (LRT) and grew its Risk & Compliance headcount by 42% (Annual Report, p. 14). Restrictions will happen more, not less.

Support is also improving on average. Its AI assistant resolves over 75% of queries, retail resolution time fell by over 40%, and support NPS rose about 12 points in 2025 (Annual Report, pp. 9, 14, 28). I believe those numbers. This study critiques the communication design around a restriction, not the compliance decision behind it.

Three insights

1. The design system is doing its job. Every product I could see in the store screens uses the same template: big balance, four round actions, then a list. That consistency is the likely reason breadth doesn't show up as clutter. A home-screen redesign would solve a problem customers aren't reporting.

2. The trust risk sits in one rare state: "my money is held". 24 of 65 negative reviews describe it, and 16 of those pair it with support complaints. Losing access to your own money weighs far more than any feature gain, and the worst week can outweigh years of good use. One reviewer, a customer for almost 10 years: "I loved them until they did this to me" (1★, US, Jun 2026).

3. The missing piece is status, not reason. Reviewers rarely demand the compliance reason. They ask when, what next, and who. Uncertain, unexplained waits feel longer (Maister, 1985), and showing the work being done raises patience (Buell & Norton, 2011). Tipping-off rules limit the why. They don't stop a bank saying what is limited, what it needs, and when you'll hear back.

How others handle it

From public store screens only; nobody shows their restricted-account state in store images, Revolut included. Monzo keeps Help as a permanent bottom tab and has a live "Call Status" screen for trust moments. N26 markets 24/7 chat as a headline feature. Wise keeps its home narrow and routes contact through in-app Help.

The strongest benchmark is internal. Revolut Business help pages describe a Home tile listing what information is requested, its status and the time remaining, with an aim to review within 3 business days (Revolut Business help). I saw that text only in a search snippet, so it needs checking. Hypothesis: the retail app has no equal status surface. I couldn't confirm this, because I don't have a restricted account to look at.

Options I considered

Option For Against Decision
A. Smarter AI that explains the restriction flow Cheap per case More bot loops in the most emotional moment; risk of tipping off Rejected
B. Case Tracker: status card, steps, "next update by" date, one case thread Fixes the most-cited gap (no timeline); reuses a Business pattern Needs honest dates from back-office tools and legal-approved copy Chosen
C. Human-first routing for every restricted account Fixes "no human" Costly per case; queue abuse Partly: route restricted accounts to a person after one bot turn

What I'd deliberately not build: a home-screen declutter or "simple mode", because the evidence doesn't support it. And nothing that explains why a restriction happened. That is a legal decision, not a design one.

The design

I built the proposal as a working, unbranded prototype (a generic banking app, not Revolut's UI), so it can be tested with real people. It has two variants: A is a simplified version of the pattern reviewers describe, B is the proposal. Names, amounts, dates and reply times are placeholders.

How I'd build it (developer's view)

  • Most of the work is backend, not UI. The screens are simple. The hard part is one reliable case-state API from back-office tooling: state, step, timestamps, next-update date, paused capabilities, requested items. I'd render the card and case page from that payload (server-driven UI), so compliance can change steps and copy without an app release.
  • Restricted mode must not lock out help. Capabilities are flags (card_payments, transfers_out, card_top_up), not one global restricted boolean, so login, the case page, uploads and the thread always work.
  • One source of truth for every channel. Each state change sends a push and an email that deep-link to the case page, both generated from the same event, so channels can't disagree.
  • Document capture: on-device blur and glare checks (Apple Vision / Google ML Kit) before upload, and resumable uploads for poor networks.
  • Ship order: the read-only card and case page with "what's paused" and "next update by" first, from data that already exists. Then the checklist. Then the thread and routing rule, which need the most back-office change.

How I'll know if it works

User test (next step). Five people who've had a bank or fintech account or payment held in the last two years, any provider. Each role-plays a declined card on variant A and variant B, in alternating order, with made-up details only. I log taps and timing in the prototype and ask: what can you still do, what do they need from you, and when will you hear back? The full script and results sheet are in test-kit.md.

Pass signal: at least 4 of 5 find the next-update date within 30 seconds on B, and at least 3 of 5 say they wouldn't contact support before that date. What would change my mind: 3 or more say the steps feel like a script hiding a decision already made, or that a moved date makes them trust it less than no date at all.

Result Variant A Variant B
Found when they'll next hear back pending pending
Correctly said what still works pending pending
Sent the right item first time pending pending
Would contact support anyway (yes) pending pending

In production, the primary metric would be support contacts per restricted case. Secondary: document rejection rate and the share of cases with 3 or more contacts. Guardrails matter as much: fraud and money-laundering detection unchanged, no longer review times, zero tipping-off incidents in copy, and complaints not rising. I'd randomise a share of new restricted cases or roll out by market, and run long enough to cover whole cases (Hypothesis: 6–8 weeks, depending on how long cases really take).

What I'd do differently, and what only Revolut knows

  • I started with a popular opinion ("super-apps get cluttered") instead of the reviews. Counting turned the project around.
  • I never saw a real restricted-account screen. The sample is "most helpful" reviews coded by one person, and store screenshots show the app at its best. The next pass needs a real capture of the flow, ideally from someone going through it.
  • Some outside figures came from search snippets because the pages blocked my fetch: the Revolut Business "time remaining" tile and the Which? Ombudsman complaint counts. I've left the Which? numbers out of this edit until I can check them.
  • Open questions only the team can answer: how many customers are restricted each month and for how long; whether back-office tools can produce a reliable "next update by" date for every restriction type; which types legally allow a step tracker at all; whether showing steps helps bad actors; and whether the retail app already has a status screen that people aren't finding. If it does, the fix is discoverability, not a new feature.
