The games aren't the problem. The hidden rules are.

Independent study. Not affiliated with or endorsed by Temu or PDD Holdings. Public sources only, every number linked. Where regulators are quoted, these are their observations, allegations or the commitments they asked for, not my legal conclusions. The proposal screens are my own concept, built as an unbranded prototype. Anything I couldn't verify is labelled Hypothesis.

Temu turned shopping into a game: spin wheels, countdowns, "free gifts" and referral challenges. When EU consumer authorities raised concerns in 2024, they did not object to the games as such. They objected to what the games hide: reward conditions, deadlines and stock claims that may not be true, and a minimum spend shown too late. Reviews say the same thing in plainer words. So I designed "Honest games": keep the game, show what it takes before the first tap, make every timer and stock claim checkable, and refund to the original payment method by default. I built it as a clickable prototype and wrote the test that would prove or kill it.

The question I started with

The question I set for this study was: where does persuasion become pressure, and what does it cost? The easy answer for Temu is "the games". The public record points somewhere narrower.

Sensor Tower ranked Temu the No. 1 shopping app by global downloads in 2025 (EcomCrew summary, Feb 2026). It depends on paid installs, so the second order decides whether acquisition pays back. Earnest Analytics found that "over 28% of Temu customers made a transaction on the platform 16 months after their first purchase", nearly double Walmart's and Target's but around half of Amazon's (Earnest, Mar 2024).

Trust is the weak point. In an April 2024 survey of 4,000 shoppers, 48% had shopped on Temu in the past year, but only 6.4% said they trust it over Amazon (Omnisend/Cint).

What the evidence shows

What regulators said. In November 2024 the EU consumer authorities (the CPC Network) listed practices they consider in breach of EU consumer law. Two matter here. One is pressure selling: "false claims about limited supplies or false purchase deadlines". The other is forced gamification: "forcing consumers to play a 'spin the fortune wheel' game to access the online marketplace, while hiding essential information about the conditions of use linked to the rewards". They also flagged a minimum order value not shown in advance (IP/24/5707). Earlier that year, Temu signed a cease-and-desist declaration in Germany after a warning letter about checkout patterns such as "Hurry! Over 126 people have this item in their cart" (vzbv, 14 May 2024).

The wording matters. The authorities targeted the hidden conditions and the unverifiable claims, not the wheel or the timer.

Two cautions. First, these are 2024 observations. Temu made commitments in Germany in May 2024 and was asked to make changes EU-wide in November 2024, so some of these patterns may have changed since. Second, the European Commission's €200 million fine in May 2026 was about illegal-product risk, not gamification. Its separate investigation into "addictive design" is still open, and I found no published outcome (IP/26/1178). I keep these strands apart.

What reviewers said. On 10 Oct 2026 I read the 43 most recent reviews on Temu's UK Trustpilot page (TrustScore 1.8 from 56,425 reviews, Trustpilot UK) and coded each one.

One reviewer quotes the app telling them their progress was "not quite enough, please make another purchase to fulfill promotional obligations" (Trustpilot UK, Oct 2026).

This sample is small and leans towards unhappy customers. The US App Store shows 4.7 from about 2.23 million ratings (App Store), so many people are satisfied. The gap is the finding. When something goes wrong, the most common complaint is a reward that cost more than it said.

Three insights

1. The lift comes from the game. The harm comes from the hidden terms. A head start towards a reward raises completion. In a car-wash field study, 34% completed a loyalty card with two free stamps versus 19% without (Nunes & Drèze, 2006). People also speed up as they near a goal (Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng, 2006). Both effects work honestly. They become manipulative when the last step secretly needs a purchase or five invites.

2. Urgency works when it can be checked. Shein, AliExpress and Amazon all use timers and stock labels too. What regulators act on is the claim that can't be checked, and the FTC treats fake countdowns and false scarcity as dark patterns (FTC, 2022). The design question isn't whether to show a timer. It's whether the timer is true.

3. Pressure wears off, and resentment builds. In experiments, mild dark patterns more than doubled sign-ups (11.3% to 25.8%) without backlash. Aggressive ones reached 41.9% but "generated a powerful backlash" (Luguri & Strahilevitz, 2021). Hypothesis: each extra pressure layer buys less conversion over time and costs more trust, which may explain part of the gap between Temu's repeat purchase and Amazon's. Public data can't prove the cause.

How others handle it

From public App Store screens: AliExpress states the condition ("with one purchase") before the user picks a welcome gift. Shein explains its flash-sale limit behind a "?", but labels its new-user credit only "Condition applies". Amazon ties Lightning Deal timers to a "% claimed" bar. All four apps use urgency. The difference is how checkable the claims are.

Options I considered

Option For Against Decision
A. Remove games and timers Simplest for the user Likely a large short-term conversion loss Hypothesis; hands the fun to rivals; won't be adopted Rejected
B. "Honest games": keep the mechanics, show the cost first, make every claim checkable Matches the regulators' exact wording; keeps the effects that work honestly Some users will skip the game once they see the cost Chosen, and test that risk
C. Leave the front end; invest only in refunds and support Helps retention Leaves the hidden-terms issue untouched Rejected, but its refund default is kept

What I'd deliberately not build: more trust badges. Temu's store listing already shows "Secure privacy | Safe payments" and "24/7 Customer Service", while 11 of the 43 reviews describe support as unreachable. A badge that the experience contradicts lowers trust.

The design

I built the proposal as a working, unbranded prototype (a generic discount shopping app, not Temu's UI), so it can be tested with real people. A is a simplified version of the pattern regulators and reviewers describe. B is the proposal. All prices, odds and dates are placeholders.

How I'd build it (developer's view)

  • Make terms required in the type system. Every promotion component (wheel, farm, coupon, timer) renders from one PromotionTerms model: value, conditions[], expiresAt, isChance, odds, source. In Flutter or SwiftUI, the widget doesn't build without it. That's a cheap guardrail across hundreds of server-driven promo modules.
  • Timers come from the server, never the device clock. Send endsAt in UTC plus server time on each response, correct for clock skew, and hide the timer if it can't be verified. Stock and sales numbers carry asOf and sourceId, so every claim can be audited later. That log is also the evidence a regulator would ask for.
  • Server-driven UI and feature flags, so each market can switch a pattern on or off without an app release.
  • Complexity: the terms sheet and the offer card are small. Truthful timers and stock are medium, because they depend on backend inventory and pricing services. The refund default is mostly product and operations work.
  • Ship first: the "What it takes" sheet and server-verified timers, in one EU market. They are the closest match to the regulators' wording and the easiest to measure.

How I'll know if it works

User test (next step). Five people who've shopped on Temu, Shein or AliExpress in the past 6 months, with a mix of ages and at least two first-time Temu users. Each goes through variant A and variant B in alternating order. I log taps and timing in the prototype and ask what the reward takes, whether the deal is good and how they know, and how much they trust the app to treat them fairly (1–7). The full script and results sheet are in test-kit.md.

Pass signal: at least 4 of 5 can state the reward's conditions and deadline without scrolling back in B, clearly more than in A, and nobody is surprised by the minimum order in B. What would change my mind: if B's sheet goes unread or misunderstood by 3 or more people, disclosure alone isn't enough and the design needs rethinking. If people understand B but trust it no more than A, the trust argument is weaker than I think.

Result Variant A Variant B

Surprised by the minimum order pending pending
Played the game / skipped it pending pending

Trust to treat me fairly (avg, 1–7) pending pending

Five people won't measure conversion. They will show whether people understand the terms, and that is the cause the A/B test then measures.

In production, the primary metric would be the share of new customers who place a second order within 90 days. Guardrails: first-order conversion (with a non-inferiority margin agreed before launch), game-related support contacts per 1,000 orders, and refund-to-credit complaints. I'd run a user-level A/B test in one EU market, roll out 5% → 25% → 50%, and keep a holdout for at least 90 days, because the effect shows up on the second order.

What I'd do differently, and what only Temu knows

  • I couldn't capture the real game flows. Mobbin required a paid plan, and Temu's store listing shows no wheels, timers or games at all. The "today" pattern comes from regulator descriptions, press accounts and reviews. The next step is to record the live first-open, game, cart and refund flows on a test device before the user test.
  • My review sample is 43 reviews from one site, coded by one person. It shows what goes wrong for unhappy customers, not how common that is.
  • Open questions only Temu can answer: what share of new buyers and sales comes through games and referrals, and so how much option B puts at risk; real game completion rates and the real cost to complete (players claim progress slows near the end, which I couldn't verify); what changed after the 2024 commitments; and whether the 4.7 vs 1.8 rating gap is about who is asked to review, and when Hypothesis.
  • The Commission's addictive-design investigation is still open, and the planned EU Digital Fairness Act may set rules in this area. Either could turn option B from a choice into a requirement.
