Temu's games aren't the problem. The hidden rules are.

Disclaimer. This is an independent case study by Mitul Vaghasiya. I am not affiliated with, paid by or endorsed by Temu, PDD Holdings, or any company named here. Everything below is either a cited public fact or is labelled Hypothesis. I had no access to Temu's internal data. Where regulators have made findings, I report what they said. I draw no legal conclusions of my own.

Temu turned shopping into a game (spin wheels, countdowns, "free gifts", credits and referral challenges) and became the most downloaded shopping app in the world in 2025. EU consumer authorities did not object to the games as such. They objected to what the games hide: the conditions attached to rewards, deadlines and stock claims that may not be true, and minimum spends shown too late. My view is that Temu can keep most of the conversion lift by making every reward state its true cost before the user plays. That is cheap to build, fair to the user, and it protects the second order, which is the one that matters.

Why this app

Scale. Sensor Tower's State of Mobile 2026 ranked Temu the No. 1 shopping app by global downloads in 2025, ahead of Shein and Amazon (EcomCrew summary of Sensor Tower, Feb 2026). Google Play shows 1B+ downloads and a 4.4 rating from about 12.9M reviews. The US App Store shows 4.7 from about 2.23M ratings (both accessed 10 Oct 2026: Google Play, App Store). In the EU, Temu declared 92 million monthly users in September 2024 (European Commission, 31 Oct 2024). Its own DSA transparency page reportedly gives about 124 million for H1 2026 (Temu DSA page; the page is rendered by script and I could not read the figure directly).

Business model. Temu is owned by PDD Holdings. PDD reported 2025 revenue of RMB 431.8 billion (about US$61.8 billion) but does not report Temu on its own (PDD 20-F for FY2025, SEC). Temu sells very low-priced goods, mostly shipped from China, and has relied on heavy paid acquisition. When the US ended the "de minimis" duty exemption on 2 May 2025, Temu cut US advertising and its US daily active users fell 58% in May, according to Sensor Tower (Reuters via Investing.com, Jun 2025).

The one metric that matters: repeat purchase. When a business depends on paid installs, the second order decides whether acquisition pays back. Earnest Analytics found that "over 28% of Temu customers made a transaction on the platform 16 months after their first purchase", which is "nearly double Walmart's and Target's" but "around half of Amazon's" (Earnest, 18 Mar 2024). Trust is the weak point. In an April 2024 survey of 4,000 shoppers in the US, UK, Canada and Australia, only 6.4% said they trust Temu over Amazon, although 48% had shopped on Temu in the past year (Omnisend/Cint).

The problem

Who is affected. New and casual shoppers, who meet the game layer first. Price-sensitive and less experienced users are the most exposed: experiments show that less-educated people are much more susceptible to mild dark patterns (Luguri & Strahilevitz, Journal of Legal Analysis, 2021). These patterns are common across e-commerce, not unique to Temu. A crawl of about 11K shopping sites found 1,818 dark-pattern instances (Mathur et al., 2019).

What goes wrong. Regulators describe the problem as a gap between what the interface promises and what it hides.

28 May 2026 European Commission €200M fine for failing to assess illegal-product risks properly. The decision does not cover the addictive-design strand, and I found no published outcome on that strand yet (IP/26/1178). Temu says it disagrees with the decision.

The fine is about product safety, not gamification. I keep the two separate. The gamification evidence comes from the CPC, vzbv and BEUC, and the DSA addictive-design question is still open.

Review mining. I read the first two pages of Temu's UK Trustpilot profile (most recent first) on 10 Oct 2026. That gave 44 reviews. I removed one duplicate text, leaving n = 43, and coded each review into one or more themes. At the time the profile showed a TrustScore of 1.8 from 56,425 reviews (Trustpilot UK).

Verbatim examples: "Shopping on TEMU felt less like normal online shopping and more like being inside a casino-style game"; "Do not fall for the timers or star games"; "not quite enough, please make another purchase to fulfill promotional obligations" (all Trustpilot UK, Oct 2026). A 2023 App Store review titled "DON'T MISS OUT" warns: "most the time when they offer free gifts you are going to have to get friends to sign up" (App Store).

The limits of this sample. Trustpilot leans towards unhappy and recent customers, and 43 reviews is a small sample. The App Store's 4.7 shows that many users are satisfied. The gap is the finding. Many people are happy with the first order, but when something goes wrong, the most common complaint is a reward that cost more than it said.

Journey audit

How I studied the screens. Mobbin's API refused access (paid plan required), so I could not load real in-app flows. The figures are Temu's and its competitors' own App Store screenshots (US store, 10 Oct 2026). These are marketing images, not live app captures. The game steps below come from regulator descriptions and first-person press accounts and are cited as such. Before this is published, the live flows will be re-captured on a test device.

One thing stands out. Temu's store listing shows no wheels, timers or games. The listing sells calm and trust. The in-app experience that regulators and users describe is louder: "spinning wheels, countdown clocks, coins, coupons and treasure chests… I was constantly winning something" (Business Day column, 2 Oct 2026).

Insights

1. The lift comes from the game. The harm comes from the hidden terms. Observation: the CPC did not call the wheel itself unlawful. It flagged forcing the wheel on users "while hiding essential information about the conditions." Evidence: 12 of 43 reviews are about rewards whose conditions appeared late. Why it matters: the endowed-progress effect means a head start towards a reward raises completion. In a car-wash field study, 34% completed with two free stamps versus 19% without (Nunes & Drèze, 2006). The goal-gradient effect means people speed up near a goal (Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng, 2006). Both work honestly. They become manipulative when the "last 1%" secretly needs a purchase or five invites.

2. Urgency works when it can be checked. Urgency that can't be checked is what regulators act on. Observation: Shein and AliExpress also use timers and stock labels (Figs. 4–6), and Amazon shows deal badges (Fig. 8). Evidence: the authorities' wording targets "false claims about limited supplies or false purchase deadlines", not timers as such. Why it matters: scarcity raises perceived value (Worchel, Lee & Adewole, 1975), and losing a claimed reward hurts more than gaining one pleases (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979). The FTC treats fake countdowns and false scarcity as dark patterns (FTC, Bringing Dark Patterns to Light, Sep 2022), and the OECD describes dark commercial patterns as design that steers, deceives, coerces or manipulates (OECD, 2022). The design question is whether the claim is true and explained, not whether to show a timer.

3. Pressure wears off, and resentment builds. Evidence: in Luguri & Strahilevitz's experiments, mild dark patterns more than doubled sign-ups (11.3% to 25.8%) without backlash. Aggressive ones nearly quadrupled them (41.9%) but "generated a powerful backlash" (JLA, 2021). The Business Day columnist says the countdown clocks soon "ceased to frighten me." Why it matters: Hypothesis. Each extra pressure layer buys less conversion over time and costs more trust, which may explain part of the gap between Temu's 16-month retention and Amazon's. I cannot prove the cause from public data.

4. Credits can turn a service failure into a trust failure. Evidence: 4 of 43 reviewers say refunds went to wallet credit without their consent ("forcefully dumped AED 80.56 into wallet credits without my consent", Trustpilot UK, Oct 2026). These are claims by users, not verified findings. Why it matters: a refund is the moment a customer decides whether to come back. A refund that feels forced into credit can damage trust in the same way a hidden reward condition does.

Benchmark

What I take from this. All four apps use urgency and incentives, so Temu is not unusual for using them. What differs is how checkable the claims are. The better patterns tie a timer to a real end time or quantity (Amazon), explain the rule in place (Shein's "?"), and state the condition before the user commits (AliExpress "with one purchase"). Amazon goes further and explains how its own reviews work. Shein's "Condition applies" shows the weak version of disclosure.

Options considered

Option User value Business value Effort Risk
A. Remove games and timers entirely High Likely large short-term conversion loss Hypothesis Low Hands the "fun" to rivals; won't be adopted
B. "Honest games": keep the mechanics, show the true cost first, make every claim checkable High Keeps most of the lift; lowers complaints and regulatory exposure Hypothesis Medium Some users will skip games once they see the cost
C. Leave front-end as is; invest only in refunds and support Medium Helps retention, but leaves the hidden-terms issue that regulators flagged High (operations) Doesn't address the CPC's "forced gamification" finding

Chosen: B, with C's refund default. It addresses the exact wording of the regulators' concerns and keeps the behavioural mechanics that work honestly.

Deliberately not building: more trust badges. Temu already shows "Secure privacy | Safe payments" and "24/7 Customer Service" (Figs. 1, 3). A badge that the experience contradicts lowers trust.

Proposed solution (wireframe level)

  1. First open: an offer card instead of a wheel gate. One card: "€X off your first order over €Y · valid until 12 Oct, 23:59". "Start shopping" and "Play for a bigger reward" carry equal visual weight. Nothing blocks the feed.
  2. "What it takes" sheet before any game. Before the first tap, the game shows: reward value, required actions (orders, minimum spend, invites), an honest estimate of time to finish, expiry, and whether the reward is guaranteed or a chance. The progress bar is linear and never slows in the last 10%.
  3. Product page: checkable claims only. "Only 5 left from this seller · updated 2 min ago" (from inventory). Crossed-out price shows "Lowest price in last 30 days: €x", in line with the EU prior-price rule (Directive 2019/2161, Art. 6a of 98/6/EC). Social-proof counts appear only if they come from live data and say what they count.
  4. Cart. The minimum order value is shown from the first item ("€6.40 more to checkout") instead of at checkout. Timers come only from server end times.
  5. Refund. The original payment method is the default. Credit is an opt-in with a stated bonus ("+5% if you take credit").
  6. Account. "Delete account" is reachable within three taps from Profile, with no retention maze.

Build note (mobile developer's view).

  • Make terms required in the type system. Every promotion component (wheel, farm, coupon, timer) renders from one PromotionTerms model: value, conditions[], expiresAt, isChance, source. In Flutter, the widget does not compile without it. That is a cheap guardrail across hundreds of server-driven promo modules.
  • Timers come from the server, never the device clock. Send endsAt in UTC plus server time on each response, correct for clock skew, and hide the timer if it can't be verified. Scarcity numbers carry asOf and sourceId so every claim can be audited later. This log is also the evidence a regulator would ask for.
  • Server-driven UI + remote config + feature flags, so each market can switch patterns on or off without an app release. That matters because EU, US and other rules differ, and App Store review cycles are slow.
  • Complexity: the terms sheet and the offer card are small. Truthful timers and scarcity are medium, because they depend on backend inventory and pricing services. Refund defaults are product and operations work more than mobile work.
  • Ship first: the "What it takes" sheet and server-verified timers in one EU market. They are the closest match to the CPC's wording and the easiest to measure.

Success metrics and validation plan

  • Primary metric: share of new customers who place a second order within 90 days.
  • Guardrails: first-order conversion (set a non-inferiority margin before launch, for example no worse than −3% relative. The real margin is a business decision), average first-order value, game-related support contacts per 1,000 orders, refund-to-credit complaints, 7-day uninstall, push opt-out rate.
  • Design: user-level A/B test in one EU market, 5% → 25% → 50% staged rollout, plus a long-term holdout of at least 90 days, because the effect shows up on the second order. Read conversion at 14 days and retention at 90 days.

5-user test script (moderated, 30 min, remote). Recruit five people who have shopped on Temu, Shein or AliExpress in the past 6 months, with a mix of ages and at least two first-time Temu users. Show prototypes A (current pattern, re-created) and B (honest) in alternating order.

  1. Warm-up: "Tell me about the last time you got something 'free' from a shopping app. What happened?"
  2. First open: "Open the app and do what you'd normally do." (Observe: do they play or skip? Time to first product view.)
  3. Comprehension: "Without scrolling back: what do you need to do to get this reward? By when?" (Success = correct conditions and deadline.)
  4. Product page: "Is this a good deal? How do you know?" (Do they notice or trust the prior-price and stock labels?)
  5. Cart: "Check out with only this €3 item." (Is the minimum spend a surprise?)
  6. Refund: "This item arrived broken. Get your money back." (Which option do they pick? Do they feel steered?)
  7. Trust rating: "On 1–7, how much do you trust this app to treat you fairly? Would you order again next month? Why?"
  8. Close: "What, if anything, felt like a trick?"

Five users won't measure conversion. They will show whether people understand the reward terms, and that is the cause the A/B test then measures.

Risks and open questions

  • What share of Temu's GMV and new buyers comes through games and referrals? Only Temu knows how much option B puts at risk.
  • Game completion rates and the real cost to complete. Players claim progress slows near the end (Factually, Jul 2026), but I could not verify it.
  • What Temu has already changed after the 2024 commitments. Some steps in the audit may be out of date.
  • The DSA addictive-design strand is still open. Its outcome, and the planned EU Digital Fairness Act (announced for Q4 2026, European Parliament Legislative Train), may set rules that make option B mandatory.
  • The 4.7 vs 1.8 rating gap probably reflects who leaves reviews and when they are asked Hypothesis. Internal NPS data would settle it.

LinkedIn spin-offs

  1. "Temu's spin wheel isn't what EU regulators objected to. The fine print behind it is." (Sides + Tension + Open loop) Visual: a redrawn wheel with one red circle on a blank "conditions" area, next to the CPC quote.
  2. "4.7 stars on the App Store. 1.8 on Trustpilot. Same app. Which number predicts the second order?" (Proof + Open loop) Visual: two big numbers side by side, with the review-theme table as the second carousel slide.
  3. "'Add a countdown timer.' Founders ask me this every month. Here's the only kind I'll build." (Tension + Sides; the "request I hear every month" pattern) Visual: Shein's timer with its "?" rule next to a timer with no source, one marked "checkable" and one marked "not checkable".

Each post resolves the same way: keep the game, show the cost, verify the claim.
