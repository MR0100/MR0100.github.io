<p class="eyebrow">Why these games exist</p>

## A daily habit, cheap growth, and browsing that becomes buying

Before redesigning a feature, I wanted to know what it does for the business. The games give people a reason to open the app every day, they grow the user base through referrals, and many tasks are “look at these items” or “place an order”. Removing them would remove all three.

<p class="eyebrow">What people say</p>

## They like the game. They resent the contract.

Of 450 App Store reviews from five countries, 111 mention the games. Several five-star reviewers play every day. The complaints cluster around specific moments: a progress bar that crawls once you’re close, a requirement for brand-new users when “everyone I know is already on Temu”, and a “free” gift that becomes a minimum spend after you’ve chosen it. One reviewer put the business case better than I could: “if the offers were clearer and simply explained, I might actually spend more.”

EU consumer authorities said something similar in 2024. They didn’t object to the wheel. They objected to hiding the conditions and to deadlines that may not be real.

<p class="eyebrow">My first design was wrong</p>

## Honest, but flat

My first version replaced the wheel with a calm offer card and a rules sheet. It fixed the contract and removed the energy the business needs. The current version keeps every bit of the game: the lights, the spin, the confetti, the countdowns. It changes only what people feel tricked by.

<p class="eyebrow">How I’ll know</p>

## Fun and fairness, measured separately

Five shoppers try today’s pattern and my version, in alternating order. The pass signal: my version scores within one point of today’s on fun, clearly higher on fairness, and four of five can state what the reward takes and by when. In production, the measure would be the second order within 90 days, with daily opens and game participation as guardrails.
