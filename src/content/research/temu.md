> **Independent study.** Not affiliated with or endorsed by Temu or PDD Holdings. Public sources only, every number linked. Where regulators are quoted, these are their observations, allegations or the commitments they asked for, not my legal conclusions. The proposal screens are my own concept, re-created in Temu's visual style for this study (no Temu logo used). Anything I couldn't verify is labelled **Hypothesis**.

**Summary.** Temu turned shopping into a game: spin wheels, countdowns, free-gift challenges and referral rewards. When EU consumer authorities raised concerns in 2024, they did not object to the games as such. They objected to what the games hide. I read 450 App Store reviews to understand both sides. People enjoy the games: several five-star reviewers play every day. What makes them angry is the contract: progress that crawls near the end, invites that only count for brand-new users, and "free" that turns into spend after they've committed. My first design fixed the contract but lost the fun. This version keeps the spin, the celebration, the countdowns and the progress, and changes only the rules: shown first, fixed, and true.

## The question I started with

The question for this study was: where does persuasion become pressure, and what does it cost? The easy answer for Temu is "the games". The evidence points somewhere narrower, and more useful.

Sensor Tower ranked Temu the No. 1 shopping app by global downloads in 2025 ([EcomCrew summary, Feb 2026](https://www.ecomcrew.com/temu-and-shein-top-the-global-shopping-app-download-charts-in-2025/)). It depends on paid installs, so the second order decides whether acquisition pays back. Earnest Analytics found that "over 28% of Temu customers made a transaction on the platform 16 months after their first purchase", nearly double Walmart's and Target's but around half of Amazon's ([Earnest, Mar 2024](https://www.earnestanalytics.com/insights/temus-retention-grows-over-time-leads-walmart-trails-amazon)).

## What the games are for

Before redesigning a feature, I wanted to know what job it does for the business. From public sources, the games do three:

1. **A reason to open the app every day.** Free-gift games like Fishland and Farmland, and daily spins, reward logging in. In Q4 2023, Temu users spent an average of 23 minutes a week in the app, against 18 on Amazon and 22 on eBay (Sensor Tower data reported by USA Today in Feb 2024, as summarised on [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temu); I couldn't open the original article, so treat this as secondary).
2. **Cheap growth through referrals.** Temu launched with Pinduoduo's "social" playbook: rewards for inviting friends. One early offer gave coupons for recruiting 7 new users, a threshold later lowered to 5 ([Tech Buzz China, Jun 2023](https://techbuzzchina.substack.com/p/temu-from-0-to-3-billion-in-10-months)).
3. **Browsing that turns into buying.** Many tasks are "look at these items" or "place an order". In Prof. Mark Griffiths' words, "you are literally having to browse to get your rewards" (BBC, Apr 2024, as quoted by [Jeu : aide et référence](https://aidejeu.ca/en/articles/temu-false-promise-jackpot/)).

How the games work, according to player guides: you pick a free prize, then fill a progress bar with coins, water or fish food. You earn those by playing daily, ordering and inviting people. Rounds often last 24 hours, and guides say progress is fast at first and slows near the end, when each step needs more invites ([Make Money Without A Job, Nov 2024, checked Feb 2026](https://makemoneywithoutajob.com/temu-games-guide/)). Temu doesn't publish the rules, so these are third-party accounts.

One more data point: Sensor Tower noted in 2023 that despite its gamified elements, Temu "still grapples with a higher churn rate" than Shein, whose loyalty game rewards logins and reviews ([Sensor Tower, Aug 2023](https://sensortower.com/blog/e-commerce-app-battle-temus-rising-star-challenged-by-sheins-loyalty-game)). Engagement alone doesn't buy loyalty.

## What regulators and reviewers say

**Regulators.** In November 2024 the EU consumer authorities (the CPC Network) listed practices they consider in breach of EU consumer law. Two matter here: "false claims about limited supplies or false purchase deadlines", and "forcing consumers to play a 'spin the fortune wheel' game to access the online marketplace, while hiding essential information about the conditions of use linked to the rewards". They also flagged a minimum order value not shown in advance ([IP/24/5707](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_24_5707)). In Germany, Temu signed a cease-and-desist declaration in May 2024 covering checkout lines such as "Hurry! Over 126 people have this item in their cart" ([vzbv](https://www.vzbv.de/pressemitteilungen/nach-vzbv-abmahnung-temu-bessert-nach)).

The wording matters: the authorities targeted hidden conditions and unverifiable claims, not the wheel or the timer. Two cautions. These are 2024 observations, and Temu has made commitments since, so some patterns may have changed. And the Commission's €200 million fine in May 2026 was about illegal-product risk, not games. Its separate "addictive design" investigation is still open, with no published outcome that I could find ([IP/26/1178](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_26_1178)).

**Reviewers.** I pulled 450 App Store reviews from five English-language storefronts (Sep 2022 – Oct 2026, most recent and most helpful), and read every one of the 111 that mention the game layer. The coding, with review ids, is in [the review coding](/studies/temu/evidence/game-themes/).

| What game-related reviews say (n = 111, one review can have several themes) | Reviews |
|---|---|
| **Progress crawls near the end** ("99.9%", "0.01% per feed", "1c away") | **17** |
| **"Free" turns into spend, or conditions appear after committing** | **14** |
| **Positive: enjoy the games, or won something** | **11** |
| Invites only count for brand-new users, or too many are needed | 11 |
| A timer ran out, or "too late" | 8 |
| The wheel and pop-ups interrupt; want a way to switch them off | 8 |

Both sides in the players' own words:

> "I like the games including which give you the opportunity to win free money to buy or the chance to win free products of your choice." (5★, New Zealand, Aug 2023)

> "you get so close to winning to best prize, but oh no! You’re a little bit short. Share to earn more!" (1★, New Zealand, May 2023)

> "it says invite new users, evreryone I know is already on Temu so how am I actually meant to win anything free!!!" (5★, New Zealand, Jun 2025)

And one that sums up the business case:

> "if the offers were clearer and simply explained, I might actually spend more." (4★, UK, Apr 2025)

A smaller, more recent Trustpilot sample (43 reviews, Oct 2026) points the same way: 12 are about promotions with conditions ([Trustpilot UK](https://uk.trustpilot.com/review/www.temu.com)).

**The surprise.** The reward people praise most isn't a game. 13 of the 450 reviews mention the automatic credit for a late delivery, or the price-drop adjustment, and 11 of them praise it, 10 at five stars. It has a clear rule, and it pays out without the customer asking.

## Three insights

**1. The engine works. The contract breaks.** A head start towards a reward raises completion. In a car-wash study, 34% completed a loyalty card with two free stamps against 19% without ([Nunes & Drèze, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1086/500480)). People speed up as they near a goal ([Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.43.1.39)). These effects are why players enjoy the games. They turn into resentment when the last 1% secretly costs a purchase or five new users, which is exactly what the 17 "crawl" reviews describe.

**2. Urgency works when it's true.** Shein, AliExpress and Amazon all use timers and stock labels. What regulators act on is the claim that can't be checked, and the FTC treats fake countdowns and false scarcity as dark patterns ([FTC, 2022](https://www.ftc.gov/reports/bringing-dark-patterns-light)). The question isn't whether to show a countdown. It's whether the deadline is real.

**3. Hidden terms cost trust, and trust drives the second order.** In experiments, mild dark patterns more than doubled sign-ups (11.3% to 25.8%) without backlash. Aggressive ones reached 41.9% but "generated a powerful backlash" ([Luguri & Strahilevitz, 2021](https://academic.oup.com/jla/article/13/1/43/6180579)). **Hypothesis:** each hidden condition buys a little conversion now and costs trust later, which may explain part of the gap between Temu's repeat purchase and Amazon's. The well-loved late-delivery credit suggests the opposite also works: clear, automatic rewards build loyalty.

## Options I considered

| Option | For | Against | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Remove games and timers | Simplest for the user | Throws away the daily habit and the fun players like; likely a large conversion loss (**Hypothesis**); won't be adopted | Rejected |
| B. Terms-first, calm version: an offer card instead of the wheel, a rules sheet before playing | Clearest disclosure | My first prototype. On review it lost the energy: no spin on open, no celebration, no countdown. It fixed the trust problem by removing the engagement the business needs | Replaced |
| C. **Keep the fun, fix the contract**: same wheel, celebration, countdowns and gift game, with rules shown first, fixed-value progress, any friend counts, true deadlines | Keeps all three business jobs; fixes the exact moments reviewers and regulators describe | Fewer people may finish the gift game once they see the real cost | **Chosen, and test that risk** |

**What I'd deliberately not build:** a game with no deadline at all. Real deadlines help people decide, and players expect them. The fix is making the deadline true and visible, not removing it.

## The design

I built it as a working prototype so it can be tested with real people. Both variants are re-created in Temu's real visual style: the shop screens (search bar, "Secure privacy | Safe payments" strip, product grid, tab bar) follow the App Store screenshots of v4.88.0 dated 10 Oct 2026, and the game screens follow dated press and guide screenshots: the spin-wheel pop-up and Fishland from Make Tech Easier (Mar 2024), the Free Gifts and Cash Reward games from a UK player guide (images from Oct 2023). Sources are listed in [`references/`](/studies/temu/sources/). No public image was found for the reward page, the product page with the cart-count notice, the cart minimum or the refund choice; those are re-created from the same visual language and the written record. **A** is a simplified version of the pattern regulators and reviewers describe. **B** is the proposal, in the same style. Both phones are live side by side in [the live prototype](/studies/temu/prototype/try.html). All prices, odds, finish rates and dates are placeholders, and every screen carries a "Re-creation for an independent study" tag.

### How I'd build it (developer's view)

- **Terms are required in the type system.** Every promotion component (wheel, gift game, coupon, timer) renders from one `PromotionTerms` model: `value`, `conditions[]`, `expiresAt`, `odds`, `stepValues[]`. In Flutter or SwiftUI, the widget doesn't build without it.
- **Progress is a list of fixed steps, not a percentage.** The server sends `steps[]` with each step's trigger and value, and the client draws segments. A step's value can't change after the game starts, so "slowing near the end" can't happen by accident or by tuning.
- **Timers come from the server.** Send `endsAt` in UTC plus server time, correct for clock skew, and hide the timer if it can't be verified. Stock and "% claimed" carry `asOf`, so every claim can be audited.
- **Automatic credits are an event, not a request.** A late-delivery job compares carrier events with the promised date and posts the credit with a push notification. It's the most-praised reward in the reviews, and it's cheap to run.
- **Ship first:** rules on the wheel and server-verified timers in one EU market (closest to the regulators' wording), then fixed-step progress for one gift game, measured against the current version.

## How I'll know if it works

**User test (next step).** Five people who've shopped on Temu, Shein or AliExpress in the past 6 months, at least two of whom have played a free-gift game. Each tries variant A and variant B, in alternating order. I log taps and timing, and after each round ask: what did you win, and what does it take to use it? How fun was it (1–7)? How fair (1–7)? Would you come back tomorrow? The script and results sheet are in [the test kit](/studies/temu/test-kit/).

**Pass signal:** B's fun score is within 1 point of A's, its fairness score is clearly higher, and at least 4 of 5 can state the reward's conditions and deadline in B. **What would change my mind:** if B feels clearly less fun, the energy isn't there yet. If people see the fixed steps and stop playing, honest progress may not hold attention, and the game needs a different reward.

**In production**, the primary metric would be the share of new customers who place a **second order within 90 days**. Guardrails that protect the business case: daily opens and game participation (the habit), first-order conversion, and referral sign-ups, each with a margin agreed before launch. Plus game-related support contacts per 1,000 orders. I'd run a user-level A/B test in one EU market, roll out 5% → 25% → 50%, and keep a holdout for at least 90 days.

## What I'd do differently, and what only Temu knows

- My first prototype was honest but flat. I solved the regulators' problem and forgot the player's. Reading the positive reviews as carefully as the negative ones would have caught that earlier.
- I couldn't capture the real game flows: Temu's store listing shows no games, and Mobbin needed a paid plan. The "today" pattern comes from regulators, player guides and reviews. The next step is recording the live first-open, game, cart and refund flows on a test device.
- My App Store sample leans towards "most helpful" reviews, which are older and longer, and it's coded by one person.
- Open questions only Temu can answer: real game completion rates and step values; what share of new buyers come through games and referrals, and so how much option C puts at risk; and what changed after the 2024 commitments.
