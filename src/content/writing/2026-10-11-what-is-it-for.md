My first design for Temu's shopping games was clean, calm and honest. I replaced the flashing spin wheel with a simple offer card and a clear rules sheet. It fixed everything people complained about in the reviews.

It was also wrong. It removed the reason millions of people open the app every day.

I've since made the same mistake in a quieter form on two other studies, and I've watched plenty of real redesigns make it too. The pattern is always the same: a team sees users struggling with a feature, designs away the struggle, and accidentally designs away the job the feature was doing for the business. Here are the three questions I now answer before I sketch anything.

## 1. What job does this feature do for the business?

Every feature that survives in a successful product is doing work, even the annoying ones. Often it's doing more than one job.

Temu's games do at least three:

- **A daily habit.** Free-gift games and daily spins give people a reason to open the app every day, even when they don't need anything.
- **Cheap growth.** Inviting friends to earn rewards brings in new users for far less than advertising.
- **Browsing that turns into buying.** Many tasks are "look at these items" or "place an order", which is exactly what the business wants.

My calm offer card kept none of those. The final design kept the wheel, the confetti and the countdowns, and changed only the rules: shown before you play, worth the same at every step, and true.

Revolut taught me a different version of the same lesson. Account holds frustrate customers, and my first instinct was to make them gentler. But holds stop fraud and meet anti-money-laundering law. Revolut says its checks saved customers an estimated £632 million in potentially fraudulent transactions in 2024. A redesign that weakened the hold would have been a disaster dressed as a kindness. The right redesign kept the hold exactly as it was and changed only what the customer could *see* while it ran.

**How to find the job:** read the company's own words. Annual reports, investor letters, engineering blogs and help pages usually say plainly why something exists. Then ask whoever owns the metric. Ten minutes with them beats a week of guessing.

## 2. What do people like about it?

Complaints are loud, so they dominate research. But a feature that frustrates some users almost always delights others, and the delight is usually the same thing the business depends on.

In 450 App Store reviews of Temu, 111 mentioned the games. Several five-star reviewers play every day. One wrote that they like *"the opportunity to win free money to buy or the chance to win free products of your choice."* That's a customer describing the habit loop in their own words.

Duolingo was the clearest case. I expected to find that the streak puts too much pressure on learners. In 127 negative reviews, **one** said the streak itself was too much pressure. The rest were from people who loved their streak and were upset to lose it. The problem wasn't the streak. It was the way back after it broke.

**How to find it:** read the five-star reviews with the same care as the one-stars, and code what people praise. Then make a short "protect list" before you make a "fix list".

## 3. What exactly do people resent?

Once you know what the feature is for and what people enjoy, the real problem usually shrinks to something specific. It's rarely "the feature". It's one moment, one rule, one missing piece of information.

- **Temu:** not the game, but progress that slows near the end ("99.99%"), invites that only count for brand-new users, and "free" that turns into a minimum spend after you've committed.
- **Revolut:** not the hold, but having no idea when it would end. In the reviews I coded, a missing timeline came up twice as often as a missing reason.
- **MyFitnessPal:** not the paywall, but a redesign that made logging the same breakfast slower than it used to be. 42 low-rated reviews blamed it.

Each of these is small enough to design for, and specific enough to test.

## The checklist I use

Before any redesign, I write three short lists:

1. **The job.** What does this feature do for the business? (With a source.)
2. **Protect.** What do users enjoy about it? (With review counts or quotes.)
3. **Fix.** What exactly do they resent? (One moment, one rule, one missing piece.)

Then every design option gets judged against all three. An option that fixes the resentment but breaks the job is out, however good it looks. An option that keeps the job and the enjoyment while fixing the resentment is usually smaller, cheaper and easier to get approved than the redesign you first imagined.

## Why this makes you more persuasive, too

There's a practical bonus. When you present a redesign to the people who own the business metric, the first question in their head is "what will this break?" If your proposal opens with "here's what this feature does for us, and here's how I've kept it", you've answered that question before they ask it.

The best redesigns I've seen don't feel like redesigns. They feel like the product, with the one thing that annoyed you quietly fixed.
