Every product team has the same research budget problem: interviews take weeks, analytics tell you *what* happened but not *why*, and surveys mostly measure who bothers to answer surveys. Meanwhile, thousands of people have already written down, in their own words, what they love and hate about your product, or your competitor's. They're sitting in the App Store and Google Play, free and public.

Most teams use them badly. Someone scrolls the one-star reviews before a planning meeting, picks the three angriest quotes and calls it research. That isn't evidence, it's a mood.

In my six case studies this year I read between a few hundred and a few thousand reviews per app. In three of the six, the reviews proved my starting idea wrong. Here's the method, step by step, so you can do the same in an afternoon.

## 1. Write your guess down before you read anything

Before opening a single review, write one sentence: "I think users struggle with X." This feels like a formality. It isn't. Without it, you'll read 500 reviews and "find" whatever you already believed.

When I studied ChatGPT, my guess was that free users couldn't find its best features. I wrote that down. Then the reviews told me that of 147 negative reviews, exactly **one** was about finding a feature, and **54** were about hitting a usage limit. If I hadn't written the guess first, I'd have quietly adjusted it as I read and never noticed I'd been wrong.

## 2. Pull a sample you can describe in one line

You need to be able to say exactly what you read. For the App Store, Apple publishes a public review feed per country:

`https://itunes.apple.com/{country}/rss/customerreviews/page={1-10}/id={app id}/sortby=mostrecent/json`

Each page returns up to 50 reviews, ten pages per country. Pull several English-speaking storefronts, remove duplicates, and keep the raw file. Google Play needs a scraper or a third-party tool, but the principle is the same.

Then write your sample line, for example: *"450 unique reviews from GB, CA, NZ, IN and IE, 21 Sep 2022 to 8 Oct 2026, 215 five-star and 150 one-star."* That line goes at the top of every finding. It's what makes the numbers checkable.

Two honest limits to state up front:

- **"Most helpful" sorting leans old.** Use "most recent" if you want today's app.
- **Reviews lean emotional.** People write when they're delighted or furious. That's fine for finding problems, and wrong for estimating how common they are among all users.

## 3. Read the five-star reviews too

This is the step almost everyone skips, and it's the one that saves you from your worst redesigns.

When I studied Temu's shopping games, the one-star reviews made the games look like a scam. The five-star reviews told a different story: people *enjoy* the spin wheel and play every day. What made them angry wasn't the game, it was the hidden rules behind it. If I'd only read the complaints, I'd have proposed removing the games, and removed the daily habit the business depends on.

Positive reviews tell you what to protect. Negative reviews tell you what to fix. You need both before you touch anything.

## 4. Code every review, one theme at a time

Coding just means tagging each review with the problems it describes. Do it in a spreadsheet:

| Review id | Rating | Date | Theme 1 | Theme 2 | Quote |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Three rules that keep the counts honest:

1. **Let themes emerge, then fix them.** Read the first 50 reviews and write themes as you go. Then freeze the list and recode from the start.
2. **One review can carry several themes.** "Crashes and support never replies" counts once for each.
3. **Keep the review id next to every count.** When someone asks "where did 17 come from?", you can show them all 17.

A keyword search speeds this up ("limit", "paywall", "streak", "crash"), but read every match in context. "No limit to how much I love this app" is not a complaint about limits.

## 5. Count, then compare the counts

Now the findings write themselves. A table like this, from my Revolut study, is worth more than any quote:

| What goes wrong (55 reviews) | Reviews |
|---|---|
| No timeline, or a long wait | 23 |
| Account closed | 13 |
| Bots, scripts, a new agent each time | 12 |
| No reason given | 11 |

My guess going in was that Revolut's app had become cluttered. In 280 reviews, clutter barely appeared. Money on hold, with no idea when it would be released, appeared again and again. And within that, people complained about the missing *timeline* twice as often as the missing *reason*. That one comparison shaped the whole design: you often can't legally tell a customer why their money is held, but you can always tell them when they'll hear back.

## 6. Quote short, quote exactly, and say where from

Pick two or three quotes that represent a theme, not the most dramatic ones. Copy them exactly, typos included, and add the rating, country and date: *"They said it would take one day to fix it. It's now up to seven days." (US, Jun 2026)*. A quote with a source reads as evidence. A quote without one reads as something you made up.

## 7. Say what the sample can't tell you

Every review study has blind spots, and naming them makes the rest more credible. A few I've had to write this year:

- KGK Diamonds, a B2B app, has only 8 public reviews. So I read 955 reviews of nine competitor apps to understand the category, and said so.
- Trade buyers almost never mention holds or memo terms in reviews. That doesn't mean they don't care; it means they settle those things on the phone. Reviews under-report anything people handle through a person.
- Counts by one person are indicative, not statistics.

## What you get for an afternoon

At the end you'll have a one-line sample, a theme table with counts and ids, a handful of sourced quotes, and a short list of limits. That's enough to change a roadmap conversation from "I feel like users hate X" to "54 of 147 negative reviews are about X, and here they are."

It won't replace talking to users. But it tells you which five users to talk to, and what to ask them.
